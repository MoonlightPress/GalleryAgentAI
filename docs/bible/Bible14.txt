LANGUAGE AND INTERNATIONALIZATION
Bible Entry 14 — Written 2026-06-03

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT COVERS

The internationalization architecture for Mochi's interface. Which
surfaces switch language, which do not, and why. How the language
toggle works across the three-companion structure. Implementation
priority order. What to avoid.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PART ONE: THE PROBLEM THIS SOLVES

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE ARTIST'S LANGUAGE SITUATION

The artist is a Chinese-speaking person living in Tokyo who submits
work to galleries and open calls in Japanese and English. Her working
day involves all three languages. Her emails shift between them. Her
professional materials exist in multiple versions. The system was
built in English, which is her third working language.

This is backwards. A system that advises an artist in her third
language is a system that keeps a slight distance. The surface
friction is small — she reads English — but the friction is there.
Analysis that arrives in Chinese lands differently than the same
analysis in English. Copy written in the language the artist thinks
in does not feel like software output. It feels like a companion
talking.

The language of the interface is not a localization detail. It is
part of the relationship between the artist and the system.

WHY THREE LANGUAGES

English, Japanese, and Chinese are not arbitrary choices.

Chinese is the language the artist thinks in. It is the language of
her interior life, her private notes, her first instinct when she
reads something. The system should be able to reach her there.

Japanese is the language of the professional environment she is
building her career in. Gallery names, venue addresses, curator
names, cultural context — all of this lives in Japanese. A
recommendation for a Tokyo gallery that arrives in Japanese feels
less like a translation and more like it belongs.

English is the lingua franca of the international art world. Open
calls from European and American institutions are written in English.
The RWS, Offprint, BOOOOOOOM — these speak English. When the artist
is thinking internationally, she is probably thinking in English.

The three languages match the three spheres of her professional life.
The toggle lets her choose which sphere she is currently operating in.

PART TWO: WHAT SWITCHES AND WHAT DOES NOT

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SURFACES THAT SWITCH LANGUAGE

Everything the system writes. Not just labels — the full text of
every surface where Mochi produces prose.

    UI chrome: navigation labels, section headers, button text,
    status messages, empty states, error messages, loading copy.

    Companion voice copy: Mochi's "I found three things" messages,
    Saffron's observation text, Peppercorn's questions. The companions
    speak in the selected language.

    Analysis sections: opportunity descriptions, match rationale,
    scoring explanations, career strategy text, recommendation
    summaries. This is the most important category. Analysis that
    arrives in the artist's primary language is analysis that she
    can absorb without translation overhead.

    Status bar: Mochi's persistent status message across all three
    pages switches with the rest.

    Actionable items: the text of Today's Focus items, Immediate Best
    Moves descriptions, watch list entries. Any prose the system
    generates as a recommendation.

    Email drafts: email drafts already exist in three languages.
    The draft displayed by default should match the current interface
    language. All three versions remain available — the language toggle
    sets the default, not an exclusive view.

SURFACES THAT DO NOT SWITCH LANGUAGE

Proper nouns. Gallery names, venue names, artist names, city names,
institution names. These are not translated. "TOKAS" is "TOKAS" in
all three languages. "Youkobo Art Space" is "Youkobo Art Space."
Translating proper nouns introduces errors and makes opportunities
harder to research.

External content. The text of an open call fetched from a third-party
website is not translated. The system can note the original language
of a source, but it does not rewrite external text.

Data fields. Dates, URLs, deadlines, submission requirements, fee
amounts. These are universal.

JSON memory. The underlying data in memory/ does not change language.
The artist profile, opportunity records, and contact memory are stored
in their source language (predominantly English) and remain that way.
Language switching is a presentation layer, not a data layer.

PART THREE: IMPLEMENTATION

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PRIORITY ORDER

The priority order is not alphabetical or the order in which the
toggle appears. It reflects the artist's actual language hierarchy.

    1. Chinese (Simplified)
    2. Japanese
    3. English

Chinese is implemented first. It is the artist's primary language.
The system in Chinese is the most useful version of the system.
Japanese follows because the professional environment is Japanese.
English is already the default — it ships for free.

IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH

Language switching is a session-level setting stored in Streamlit
session state. On first load, the default is Chinese. The artist
can toggle to Japanese or English. The selection persists for the
session and is written to a user preference file so it persists
across sessions.

The toggle lives in the persistent header or sidebar — visible from
all three companion pages. It does not belong to any single companion.
It is infrastructure, not content.

All system-generated prose passes through a language-aware text
layer before rendering. This layer:

    — accepts a content key or template string
    — looks up the appropriate translation
    — falls back to English if the translation is missing
    — never silently renders an untranslated key

The translation layer is not a machine translation wrapper.
Translations are written strings, not generated on the fly. The
companion voice is the most important surface — Mochi, Saffron, and
Peppercorn should sound like themselves in each language, not like
a translated script. Their voices are assets to be written, not
strings to be processed.

For analysis sections generated by Claude API calls, the language
parameter is passed in the prompt. The model generates analysis
directly in the target language. This is preferred over translating
English output after the fact — direct generation in Chinese or
Japanese produces better prose.

TRANSLATION SURFACE INVENTORY

Before implementation begins, produce a complete inventory of every
string that must be translated. Categories:

    Companion voice lines (Mochi, Saffron, Peppercorn)
    Status bar messages
    Section and subsection headers
    Empty state copy
    Error and loading messages
    Button and action labels
    Today's Focus structure labels (Quick Win, High Impact Move,
    Stretch Goal)
    Analysis section headers
    Tier labels and descriptions
    Career strategy copy
    Scoring rationale templates

This inventory is the scope of the translation work. It will be
larger than expected. Do not underestimate it.

THE TOGGLE IN CONTEXT OF THE THREE COMPANIONS

Language is not a companion-specific setting. Mochi does not speak
Chinese while Peppercorn speaks Japanese. All three companions speak
the same language because they are all part of the same system
talking to the same artist.

The toggle is available on all three pages because the artist may
want to change language while reading Saffron's market observations
or while answering Peppercorn's questions. The transition between
languages should be immediate and feel like flipping a register, not
reloading a page.

PART FOUR: WHAT TO AVOID

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

DO NOT MACHINE-TRANSLATE THE COMPANION VOICE

The companion voice is the most carefully designed element of the
interface. Mochi does not say "I have found the following items
for you." She says "I found these." Peppercorn does not say "Please
provide your artist statement." He says "I was wondering."

If machine translation is used on this copy without curation, the
voice is lost. The companions become generic software output that
happens to be in Chinese. This is worse than leaving it in English —
at least English copy sounds intentional.

The companion voice must be written by a native speaker or carefully
reviewed by one. It is not a localization cost to minimize. It is
core product quality.

DO NOT DEFAULT TO ENGLISH WITHOUT A PREFERENCE CHECK

On first load, English is not the right default. Chinese is the
right default for this artist. If the system is ever deployed for
other artists, the default language should be determined by artist
profile, not by what is easiest to ship.

An artist who opens a system that defaults to her third language
will change the setting once and never think about it again — but
the first impression was wrong, and first impressions leave marks.

DO NOT TREAT THIS AS COSMETIC

The temptation is to treat language switching as a UI polish feature:
something to add after the important work is done. This framing is
wrong. For this specific artist, Chinese is not a cosmetic preference.
It is the difference between a system that feels like her own and a
system that feels borrowed.

Language switching belongs in the current development phase. It is
not a stretch goal. It is not Tier 4.

DO NOT PARTIALLY TRANSLATE

A partially translated interface is worse than an untranslated one.
If the headers are in Chinese but the analysis is in English, the
mixed-language experience reads as broken, not multilingual. The
fallback to English for missing translations is an engineering
safety net, not a design choice. Every string in the inventory must
be translated before a language is marked as available in the toggle.

PART FIVE: RELATION TO OTHER BIBLE ENTRIES

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Bible08 defined the three-companion architecture. The language toggle
is a layer on top of that architecture, not a change to it. The
companions are who they are regardless of language. Mochi is still
purposeful and feline. Saffron is still patient and wide-seeing.
Peppercorn is still intimate and careful. Language is how they
speak, not who they are.

Bible13 defined the desk metaphor — that the artist is always at her
desk and the companions come to her. The language toggle is the
artist's right as the owner of the desk. The companions speak her
language because they are guests. Guests adapt. The desk belongs to
her.

The email drafting system already generates in three languages. This
spec catches the rest of the interface up to the standard that email
drafts already meet.
