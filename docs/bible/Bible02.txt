# MOCHI BIBLE

## Volume II — Development History

### The Discovery Era

The first version of Mochi was fundamentally a discovery engine.

The assumption was simple:

If artists knew about more opportunities, they would have better careers.

The earliest work focused on collecting information.

The system searched for:

* galleries
* contests
* open calls
* publications
* residencies
* book fairs
* artist organizations

The initial goal was scale.

More sources.

More opportunities.

More recommendations.

The system succeeded at this.

It became increasingly capable of finding opportunities.

Unfortunately, usefulness did not increase at the same rate.

This became the first major lesson.

Discovery and usefulness are not the same thing.

A database can be large while remaining unhelpful.

An artist does not benefit from finding one hundred opportunities if ninety-five of them are poor matches.

This insight would eventually drive almost every major architectural decision that followed.

---

### The Ranking Era

The obvious response to discovery overload was ranking.

If there were too many opportunities, perhaps they could simply be sorted.

Scoring systems were introduced.

Opportunities received scores based on relevance.

The assumption was:

Discovery plus ranking equals value.

At first this appeared reasonable.

The system became increasingly sophisticated.

Scores improved.

Reports improved.

Rankings became more detailed.

Yet something remained wrong.

The highest ranked opportunities often did not feel right.

The machine was identifying relationships.

It was not understanding the artist.

This distinction became critical.

The system could identify similarity.

It could not identify fit.

---

### The Artist DNA Breakthrough

Artist DNA was one of the first major breakthroughs.

Instead of focusing primarily on opportunities, the project began focusing on the artist.

This represented a philosophical shift.

The question changed from:

"What opportunities exist?"

to:

"Who is this artist?"

Image analysis became central.

The goal was to build a machine-readable representation of an artist's visual identity.

The system began extracting:

* medium
* subjects
* themes
* moods
* recurring motifs
* aesthetic tendencies

This immediately improved recommendation quality.

For the first time, recommendations were being filtered through the identity of the artist rather than through generic categories.

This became one of the strongest systems in the project.

Many later systems depended upon it.

---

### The Photography Disaster

This was one of the most important failures in the history of the project.

The artist was a watercolor painter.

Yet recommendations repeatedly drifted toward:

* photography magazines
* photography contests
* photography galleries
* photobooks
* zines

The machine was not entirely wrong.

The artist's work shared characteristics with photography.

Urban scenes.

Architecture.

Atmosphere.

Memory.

Observation.

The system detected these similarities.

The problem was that it ignored medium.

As a result:

Urban watercolor became urban photography.

Architecture painting became architecture photography.

Atmosphere became photobooks.

The machine was recognizing aesthetic overlap while ignoring artistic practice.

This created a flood of recommendations that looked reasonable but felt wrong.

The project spent significant effort understanding this problem.

This single failure eventually produced multiple architectural layers.

---

### Watercolor Correction

The Watercolor Layer emerged directly from the photography problem.

Its purpose was simple:

Protect painters from being overwhelmed by photography.

The system began explicitly accounting for medium.

Watercolor became meaningful.

Painting became meaningful.

The recommendation engine started asking:

"What kind of artist is this?"

instead of merely asking:

"What subjects appear in the work?"

This dramatically improved results.

Painting opportunities became visible again.

Many later successes can be traced back to this correction.

---

### Source Purity

A second problem emerged.

Even after improving medium matching, recommendations remained noisy.

Many highly ranked results were not opportunities.

Examples included:

* book listings
* catalogs
* social media pages
* informational resources
* archive pages

The system had become skilled at finding things related to opportunities.

It was less skilled at finding actual opportunities.

This distinction became known as Source Purity.

The project began distinguishing:

Opportunity

from

Information about opportunity.

This sounds trivial.

It was not.

The distinction became foundational.

Many later ranking improvements relied upon it.

---

### Truth Alignment

As ranking systems became more sophisticated, a new problem emerged.

Score inflation.

Everything began looking good.

Many opportunities received high scores.

Many reports appeared convincing.

Yet reality frequently disagreed.

The project realized something uncomfortable.

A recommendation system can become increasingly confident while becoming increasingly wrong.

Truth Alignment emerged as a corrective mechanism.

The goal was to anchor recommendations to evidence.

The system became increasingly skeptical.

Evidence became more important than theory.

Verification became more important than prediction.

This represented one of the largest philosophical shifts in the project.

---

### The Verification Revolution

Eventually a fundamental realization occurred.

A perfect ranking is worthless if the opportunity no longer exists.

A perfect ranking is worthless if submissions are closed.

A perfect ranking is worthless if contact information cannot be found.

Verification became unavoidable.

The project began pursuing:

* website verification
* contact discovery
* deadline discovery
* submission discovery
* application requirement discovery

This was the beginning of a new phase.

The project was no longer merely interpreting information.

It was attempting to validate reality.

This remains one of the least mature systems.

It is also one of the most important.

---

### The Shift Toward Career Thinking

As the project matured, the focus expanded.

Opportunity discovery alone was insufficient.

Artists need strategy.

Artists need prioritization.

Artists need sequencing.

Artists need relationship management.

The project began moving toward a broader question:

"How does an artist build a career?"

This was the beginning of the Career Layer.

The eventual goal became:

* daily actions
* weekly goals
* monthly priorities
* long-term planning

The project started moving away from databases and toward decision support.

---

### Report Explosion

As development accelerated, the number of reports multiplied.

Every new idea produced:

* reports
* rankings
* summaries
* dashboards

This created a new problem.

Information overload within the project itself.

The system designed to reduce complexity began generating complexity.

This became a recurring theme.

A lesson emerged:

Reports are easy to generate.

Clarity is difficult.

This lesson remains relevant.

---

### Patch Accumulation

Rapid experimentation produced many patches.

Each patch solved a real problem.

Collectively they created technical debt.

Over time the project accumulated:

* alternate runners
* alternate reports
* alternate scoring systems
* temporary fixes
* experimental branches

The codebase became increasingly difficult to understand.

Not because it lacked capability.

Because it contained history.

---

### The Context Crisis

Eventually the greatest challenge was no longer technical.

The challenge became understanding.

The project had reached a point where:

The creator understood the system.

The repository did not explain itself.

The architecture existed.

The reasoning behind the architecture was scattered across conversations, experiments, reports, and decisions.

This realization directly motivated migration efforts.

The goal was no longer simply preserving code.

The goal was preserving institutional memory.

Without that memory, future systems would likely repeat old mistakes.

---

### Current Position

At present, Mochi sits between two stages.

It is no longer an opportunity finder.

It is not yet a complete career operating system.

The strongest areas are:

* discovery
* artist DNA
* painting alignment
* recommendation logic

The weakest areas are:

* verification
* CRM
* relationship management
* action planning

The next phase is not about finding more opportunities.

The next phase is about becoming more useful.
