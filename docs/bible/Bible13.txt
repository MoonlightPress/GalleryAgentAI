THE DESK METAPHOR
Bible Entry 13 — Written 2026-06-02

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT COVERS

The psychological model behind the three-page architecture. Not the
technical structure, not the data flow — the felt experience of using
the system. Why the artist never feels like she is navigating software.
Why each companion feels different from the others without requiring
explanation. Why the desk is the right image.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PART ONE: THE ARTIST IS ALWAYS AT HER DESK

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE CORE CLAIM

The artist does not go anywhere. The companions come to her.

This is not a metaphor layered onto an existing structure after the
fact. It is the structure. Everything about the three-page architecture
— why there are three pages, why they feel different, what each page's
job is — follows from this single image.

The artist is at her desk. She is working, or she is thinking about
working, or she is procrastinating. The desk is her place. It has her
materials on it, her calendar, her coffee. The window is open. The
baseboards are old wood with small gaps. This is where she lives her
working life.

The system does not ask her to go somewhere. It does not ask her to
open a dashboard, enter a workspace, consult an interface. Those are
all words for leaving the desk and entering a different kind of room —
a room that belongs to the software, not to her. The desk belongs to
her. The companions visit. She stays.

WHY THIS MATTERS

The alternative — the dashboard model — puts the artist inside the
software. She becomes a user navigating a product. Her attention is
managed by tabs, menus, information architecture. The system is in
charge of the space.

The desk model reverses this. She is in charge of the space. The
companions arrive when they have something. She can pay attention to
them or not. They do not demand. They bring things and wait.

This is the correct psychological relationship between an artist and a
support system. She has the work. The system has information that may
or may not be useful to her right now. The system should make itself
available without making itself important.

PART TWO: THE THREE COMPANIONS AT THE DESK

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOCHI — THE CAT IN THE WINDOW

Mochi is a grey tabby. She jumps through the open window and drops
things on the desk.

This is not a gentle arrival. It is feline and purposeful. Mochi has
been out — she has been hunting, searching, finding — and she returns
with something in her mouth. She puts it down in front of the artist
and waits to see what happens. Then she sits nearby and watches.

What she brings: opportunities. Specific, actionable, ranked. Mochi
has done the legwork. She has been through the city at night — the
open calls, the gallery newsletters, the submission deadlines — and she
has brought back the things that matter. Three things. Not fifty.

The things on the desk are ready to act on. This is what distinguishes
Mochi from the other companions. She does not report. She does not
observe. She delivers. The question her page answers is: what should I
do today?

When the artist sits down and looks at the desk, Mochi's offerings are
the first things she sees. They are concrete. They have deadlines. They
have addresses. They have draft emails that are almost ready to send.
The work of deciding and researching has already been done. The artist's
job is to act or not act. That is all.

Mochi is the most action-oriented of the three companions. She is not
interested in context or history or long-term thinking. She is
interested in the next thing. She has no patience for hesitation but
no anxiety either — she has done her job, the opportunity is on the
table, what happens next is not her department.

The warmth of Mochi's page — the cream and amber, the aged paper — is
the warmth of a working studio in the afternoon. This is where things
get done.

SAFFRON — THE BIRD ON THE WINDOWSILL

Saffron is a red or yellow bird. She lands on the windowsill and
chirps observations.

She does not come inside. She stays at the edge — perched where she
can see both out and in, where she can report on the wider world
without disrupting the desk. Her visit is quieter than Mochi's. She
did not hunt. She watched. She has been very high up, and from up
there she saw patterns that are invisible from the desk.

What she brings: context. The shape of the landscape. What comparable
artists are doing. What the market looks like from altitude. Where the
opportunities cluster. What the statistics say about careers at this
stage. Saffron does not advise — she describes. She is scrupulous about
this distinction. She reports what she saw. What the artist does with
it is the artist's business.

Saffron's chirps are not urgent. She is not bringing a deadline. She
is not saying do this now. She is saying: from up there, here is what
I noticed. Take it or leave it. She will still be on the windowsill
tomorrow.

This quality — patient, long-horizon, non-interventionist — is why
Saffron's page is for reading, not for acting. The artist comes to
Saffron when she wants to understand the bigger picture, not when she
wants to know what to do next. Saffron never tells her what to do.

The bird does not come inside partly because she could not fit at the
desk — she sees too much, her information is too wide, her field of
view is the whole city not the current project — and partly because she
respects that the desk belongs to the artist. She offers what she has
from where she stands.

PEPPERCORN — THE MOUSE IN THE BASEBOARD

Peppercorn is a black mouse. He lives in the mousehole in the
baseboard. The desk is his home too.

Of the three companions, Peppercorn is the most intimate. He did not
arrive from somewhere else. He has always been here. He has been
watching the artist work for months. He knows which stacks on the desk
she returns to and which she ignores. He notices when she stays late.
He has heard her muttering to herself about shows she wants to apply
for and problems she is not sure how to solve.

What he wants: to know more. Peppercorn does not bring things to the
desk. He asks questions from beside it. Small, careful questions that
he has thought about for a while before asking. Questions whose answers
would help him understand her better. Questions that, once answered,
would make Mochi's deliveries more accurate and Saffron's observations
more relevant.

He also notices things. He notices that the artist's statement has not
been updated in six months. He notices that she has mentioned Tokyo
Art Book Fair three times without doing anything about it. He does not
say: you should do this. He says: Peppercorn noticed. Worth a thought?

Peppercorn's page is the only page where the artist's voice enters the
system. Without it, the system is a monologue — two companions
delivering information to an artist who has never been asked what she
wants. Peppercorn closes this loop. He is the system listening.

The cozy corner aesthetic of his page — the sage green, the small
scale, the intimate typography — is the aesthetic of the baseboard
world. Not small as in unimportant. Small as in close. Small as in
everything in its place, quiet attention, long memory.

PART THREE: NAVIGATION IS ATTENTION, NOT TRAVEL

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE WRONG MODEL

The wrong model for navigation is rooms. Three rooms in a house, each
belonging to a different companion. The artist walks from one room to
another. She enters Mochi's room, then Saffron's room, then
Peppercorn's room.

This model is wrong because it moves the artist. She leaves her desk.
She is now in the software's space, not her own. Each page becomes a
destination rather than a companion. The experience becomes
administrative — going somewhere to do something, rather than paying
attention to whoever is nearby.

The wrong model also implies that only one companion is present at a
time. That the others disappear when she is not looking at them. That
each visit is a complete and separate event.

THE RIGHT MODEL

The right model is focus. The artist is at her desk. All three
companions are present at all times — Mochi on the desk with her
offerings, Saffron on the windowsill, Peppercorn at the baseboard.
Navigation between pages is not travel. It is the artist deciding
which companion to pay attention to right now.

She is not going anywhere. She is turning her head.

When she looks toward the desk, she sees what Mochi brought. When she
looks toward the window, she hears what Saffron noticed from above.
When she looks toward the baseboard, she sees what Peppercorn has left
out for her — or she answers one of his questions. Then she looks back
at her work.

This model preserves the continuity of the workspace. It explains why
Mochi's status bar persists across all three pages — because Mochi is
always on the desk even when the artist is talking to Peppercorn. The
status bar is Mochi's ambient presence. She is still there. She is
just not the current focus.

This model also explains why there are three companions and not four,
not two. Three is the right number of things a person can hold in
peripheral awareness while working. One is too few — there is no
texture. Two is sparse. Four becomes background noise. Three feels
like a lived-in space.

PART FOUR: IMPLICATIONS FOR DESIGN

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE DESK IS NOT THE INTERFACE

The interface should never feel like the primary environment. It should
feel like a window into a slightly magical version of the artist's
own studio. She recognizes the space. It belongs to her. The companions
are guests — welcome guests, trusted guests, but guests.

This has a practical consequence: the interface should never expand to
fill the screen in a way that makes the artist feel contained by it.
It should feel like looking at something, not being inside something.
Modals, full-screen takeovers, and complex navigation hierarchies all
work against this. They make the software feel like a room.

THE COMPANIONS HAVE ROLES, NOT FEATURES

The desk metaphor clarifies what each page is for and, equally
importantly, what it is not for.

Mochi's page is for action. It does not belong to context or history
or statistics. Those belong to Saffron. Putting career statistics on
Mochi's page would be like Mochi sitting at the desk giving a lecture
instead of dropping something to act on. It is wrong for her character.

Saffron's page is for observation. It does not belong to action items
or deadlines or draft emails. Those belong to Mochi. Saffron reports
what she saw. She does not place things on the desk. She has no hands.

Peppercorn's page is for listening. It does not belong to analysis or
recommendations. Those emerge from what Peppercorn knows, but they
belong to Mochi and Saffron. Peppercorn's page is the input channel,
not the output channel. He is the one asking. Not the one telling.

When a feature is proposed, the question is always: which companion
does this belong to? If the answer is unclear, the feature does not
have a clear role. A feature that could belong to any of the three
companions probably belongs to none of them.

THE ARRIVAL MATTERS

Each companion has a characteristic mode of arrival. Mochi jumps
through the window — sudden, purposeful, physical. Saffron lands on
the sill — lighter, quieter, external. Peppercorn is already there —
he did not arrive, he has been here, he emerged from the baseboard
when the moment was right.

These different modes of arrival should be felt in how each page
opens. Mochi's page should feel like something has been delivered —
the content is ready and waiting. Saffron's page should feel like
looking out — wide, contextual, patient. Peppercorn's page should feel
like settling in — cozy, close, unhurried.

This is a design brief for animation, loading states, and first
impressions. It has not been implemented yet. But the model is here
so that when it is implemented, it is implemented correctly.

THE ARTIST NEVER ASKS FOR HELP

This is a subtle but important implication of the desk metaphor.
In the desk model, the artist is the primary presence. She is working.
The companions offer things. She accepts or ignores.

The interface should never frame itself as helping the artist. Helping
implies that she needed help and asked for it. She did not ask for it.
The companions noticed she was working and brought what they thought
might be useful.

This distinction shapes every piece of copy in the system. Mochi does
not say: here are some opportunities for you. She says: I found these.
Saffron does not say: let me show you the market. She says: from up
here, I noticed. Peppercorn does not say: please complete your profile.
He says: I was wondering.

The artist retains all the agency. The companions have all the
initiative. This is the correct distribution.
