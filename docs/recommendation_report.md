# Recommended Collaborators
Give 3 artists worth researching immediately.

**1. Zhang Kechun (Photography, Beijing)**
Research him first because he's your clearest complementary peer—same subjects (Chinese vernacular, disappearing neighborhoods), same palette (muted, atmospheric), different medium (photography vs. painting). This matters because galleries love pairing photographers and painters on the same theme—it creates exhibition depth without competition. If you ever approach a curator, saying "I paint what Zhang Kechun photographs" is an instant conceptual anchor.

**2. Kazuhiro Hori (Painting, Japan)**
He's your technical twin—watercolor/gouache, everyday domestic observation, quiet interiors, decorative appeal without apology. Research him because he's solved the problem you're facing: how to be taken seriously while making beautiful, accessible work. Study: his exhibition history (does he show in "serious" galleries or illustration-adjacent spaces?), how he prices work, whether he does editions. His career path is a potential model for yours. If he's successfully navigated the "too decorative" critique, learn how.

**3. Attasit Pokpong (Painting, Bangkok)**
He's your thematic parallel in a different city—Thai shophouses and vernacular preservation with the same elegiac tone. Research him because regional group shows often want geographic range while maintaining thematic coherence. If a curator is building "Disappearing Asian Vernacular" and already has a Chinese artist, they need the Thai complement. Study: does he show regionally (Bangkok, Singapore) or internationally? Does he frame his work as preservation activism or pure observation? Knowing his positioning helps you differentiate or align strategically.

# Low-Effort Wins
Give 5 small things the artist could do this week.

**1. Add "Available as prints" to your social media bio (5 min)**
If you have Instagram/website, add one line: "Originals and prints available—DM for info." This is pre-selling before you've set up infrastructure. When someone asks, it forces you to figure out pricing/logistics. The asking itself is market research. No need to have print-on-demand set up yet—just test if anyone wants it.

**2. Google "your city + contemporary realism gallery" (10 min)**
Search "[your city name] contemporary realism gallery" and "[your city] figurative painting gallery." Click on 3 results. Look at one current exhibition from each. Save the gallery names in a note. You're building a target list of local spaces that might eventually show your work, or at least tell you what's realistic in your area. Zero commitment, pure reconnaissance.

**3. Screenshot your 5 most-liked images from social media (8 min)**
Go through your Instagram/social posts. Note which 5 images got the most engagement (likes, saves, comments). Screenshot them into a folder labeled "Audience favorites." These aren't necessarily your best work, but they're what strangers respond to—critical data for choosing what to upload to Saatchi, what to print, what to lead with in submissions.

**4. Write one sentence about why you paint doorways (12 min)**
Open a note and complete: "I keep painting doorways/thresholds because ___________." Be honest, not poetic. "Because they're compositionally easy" is fine. "Because they remind me of my grandmother's house" is fine. This becomes the seed of your artist statement. The dossier noted your work's sincerity—start there, not with art-speak. Save this sentence.

**5. Price-check one comparable painting on Artsy (10 min)**
Go to Artsy.net, search "contemporary watercolor urban" or "Asian domestic painting." Find ONE work that's similar in size/subject to yours. Note: artist name, price, gallery, size. Write it down. You're calibrating your mental price scale against the real market. Do this once now, once a month. Eventually you'll know exactly where you fit.

# Long-Term Strategic Directions
Give 3 larger trajectories worth developing over 1-3 years.

**1. Become the definitive painter of [specific architectural typology]**
Instead of "Asian urban vernacular" (too broad), own one typology: Beijing courtyard doorways, Shanghai longtang alleys, Hong Kong tong lau staircases, or Taipei lane houses. Spend 1-3 years painting ONLY that, researching its history, exhibiting under that frame. Why this matters: Specificity beats generality in art markets. "The artist who paints Beijing courtyards" is collectible and curatorially useful. "The artist who paints various Asian scenes" is not. Zhang Kechun owns the Yellow River; Liu Xiaodong owns migration sites. You need yours. This doesn't limit you—it focuses you until you're known for something. Then you can expand.

**Milestones:**
- Year 1: Research and document your chosen typology (100+ reference photos, historical reading)
- Year 2: Produce 30-50 works exclusively on this subject, develop exhibition proposal
- Year 3: Pitch solo show to galleries as "The [Typology Name] Project"—a documentary-artistic archive

**2. Develop a dual practice: precious originals + accessible editions**
Stop thinking "original vs. print" as hierarchy—build two parallel markets. Precious track: 10-15 large-scale watercolors annually at $5,000-15,000 for galleries/collectors. Accessible track: Signed limited editions at $200-500 online, open prints at $40-80. Why this matters: Sustainability comes from multiple income streams. Illustration-adjacent artists (your actual peers) survive on volume sales while building gallery credibility. Korean artist Minjae Lee does this successfully—museum shows AND Society6 presence, no shame. You can too.

**Milestones:**
- Year 1: Set up ONE print-on-demand platform, test 10 images, track sales data
- Year 2: Produce first limited edition with proper giclée printer (50 prints, 2-3 images), sell half
- Year 3: Have 2-3 gallery relationships for originals + established online print business generating $15,000-30,000 annually

**3. Position as preservation documentarian, not nostalgic decorator**
Shift framing from "I paint pretty old buildings" to "I document threatened typologies before erasure." This means: research the architecture you paint (interview residents, cite demolition dates, note historical context), write extended captions, consider publishing a book, partner with preservation orgs. Why this matters: It upgrades you from "decorative realist" to "artist-documentarian"—institutional exhibitions, grants, residencies become possible. You're still painting the same subjects, but the frame changes who takes you seriously.

**Milestones:**
- Year 1: Start extended captions for each work (architecture history, personal story, current status)
- Year 2: Publish photo essay or zine combining your paintings with documentation text
- Year 3: Propose exhibition to architecture museum or cultural heritage center, partner with preservation nonprofit for visibility

**Why these three directions work together:** They let you stay emotionally true (quiet observation, same subjects) while building market viability (specific niche, multiple income streams, institutional legitimacy). You don't have to become more conceptual or political—just more focused and strategic about the work you're already making.
