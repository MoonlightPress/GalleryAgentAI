# Five futures — draft

Working draft for the section that replaces 长期路径. English only; Chinese and
Japanese come after the content is agreed.

**The principle.** Not "which strategy should you take" — she can't choose which
door says yes, and she's already doing several of these at once. These are
*kinds of success*, named for the thing that actually defines the life, with the
money made explicit and the next moves specific enough to act on without asking
anyone's permission.

**What each one contains:** the life, how the money works, paint-by-numbers
steps, where she already stands, the chart that belongs there, and — last and
small — someone living it.

Her position across these is wildly uneven, and that asymmetry is the whole
point. She is a long way along one of them and at zero on another.

---

## 1. No Gatekeepers
*The audience is the asset. Nobody's permission required.*

**The life.** You post the work, the following grows, and the following is the
business. No gallery takes a cut, no publisher decides your next book, no client
sends notes. Income comes from the audience directly — prints, editions, a
course, a membership. The risk isn't rejection, it's that it all rests on you
continuing to show up. The upside is that nothing can be taken away by someone
else's decision.

**How the money works.** Three stacked layers, in ascending order of how much
they pay and how much work they are up front:
- **Prints and postcards** — she has these (¥2,200). Low margin, constant.
- **Editions and originals** — ¥31,900–115,500. High margin, low volume.
- **A course** — made once, sold indefinitely. This is the layer she doesn't
  have, and it's the one that turns an audience into a living. Domestika and
  Skillshare classes by illustrators are a standard income category; a
  self-hosted course keeps far more per sale but sells to your own list only.

**Paint by numbers.**
1. Pick the one thing you know that people repeatedly ask you about. For an
   architectural watercolorist that is almost always *how you handle
   perspective and light on buildings*, not "how to paint."
2. Record it once. A course is 3–5 hours of video, not a curriculum.
3. Price it at roughly the cost of one small print run to the buyer — the market
   for illustrator courses sits far below what a workshop would cost in person.
4. Launch to the people already following the work. No new audience required.
5. It sells while you sleep, or it doesn't — either way you find out in a month
   rather than a year.

**Where she stands.** Furthest along of any of these. Six years of daily work,
~26k people who already watch it, a shop that already functions, eight zines and
a book. The missing piece is the top layer, and it's the only one that scales.

**Chart:** revenue per hour of work, by layer — prints vs editions vs a course,
where a course is a single upfront cost against indefinite sales.

**Someone living it:** Mateusz Urbanowicz — Tokyo streets in watercolor,
self-published, direct audience, no gallery.

## 2. Someone Else Sells It
*A gallery does the selling, the pricing, and the finding of buyers.*

**The life.** The defining difference from now isn't prestige, it's that **the
money flows the other way**. Today she pays for the room — ¥340,000 for twelve
days at Le Monde, and they keep 30% of what sells. In this life the gallery
pays for the show, takes a commission, and its job is to find collectors who buy
again. Prices per painting rise because someone else is arguing for them.

**How the money works.** Commission replaces rent. A representing gallery takes
40–50% — more than Le Monde's 30% — but you pay nothing up front, they cover
production and promotion, and they bring the buyers. The comparison that matters:
- **Now:** ¥374,000 out of pocket, then 70% of whatever sells.
- **Funded open call:** TOKAS pays ¥150,000 *toward* the work, venue free.
- **Representation:** ¥0 out, 50–60% of a higher price, to people who return.

**Paint by numbers.**
1. Ten paintings that hang together as one body. Every funded door and every
   gallery conversation starts here, and it's the same ten for all of them.
2. Apply to the venues that pay you rather than charge you — TOKAS-Emerging
   (opens ~June), OPEN SITE (~February), Kyoto Co-program (~October).
3. Visit the galleries whose roster looks like your work. Go as a viewer. Twice.
4. Then write — one email that introduces you and asks what they'd want to see.
   It doesn't ask for a show, so there's nothing to decline, and the reply is a
   specification you can build toward.
5. Repeat with ten galleries. One of them answers.

**Where she stands.** Eight group shows, two museum group shows, three solos,
an international showing. The record is real; what's missing is a show someone
else paid for, and that's the single credential that changes the conversation.

**Chart:** the same show, three ways — self-funded rental vs funded open call vs
represented, as money in and money out.

**Someone living it:** Keita Morimoto — nocturnal Tokyo streets, at a Tokyo
gallery that sells for him.

## 3. The Work Goes Out Without You
*Your images on things, made and sold by other people.*

**The life.** Quiet money. You license images — to stationery, homeware,
packaging, book covers, brands — and other companies manufacture, distribute and
sell them. You are not famous in this life, necessarily. You are paid repeatedly
for work you already made. It suits someone who wants to keep painting and not
manage anything.

**How the money works.** Two shapes:
- **Flat fee** — one payment for one use. Simple, capped.
- **Royalty** — a percentage of sales, ongoing, unbounded, and the reason this
  category can outpace every other one on this list.
Architecture and cityscape work licenses differently from character work: the
buyers are interiors, hospitality, stationery, publishing and travel rather than
toys and apparel.

**Paint by numbers.**
1. Assemble a set of images that work small and repeat — licensing buys pattern
   and mood, not single masterpieces.
2. Register the work properly so a royalty is enforceable.
3. Approach the categories that already use this imagery: stationery makers,
   hotel and café interiors, book publishers needing covers.
4. Never sign an unlimited flat-fee buyout on a body of work you'd want to
   license again. This is the one place where a bad first contract costs years.

**Where she stands.** Effectively zero, and it's the largest untouched category
on this list. It's also the one least dependent on where she lives or what visa
she holds.

**Chart:** flat fee vs royalty over five years, at a plausible unit volume —
showing where a royalty overtakes a one-off payment.

who the right architectural-watercolor licensing example is.)*

## 4. Between Covers
*The book is the work, not a record of it.*

**The life.** Books are the vehicle. Not zines documenting the practice — books
that people buy because they're books. A publisher may be involved or not; the
self-published route keeps far more per copy and demands that you do everything.
She has said she wants another book at 30. The material for a hardcover exists
already: six years of daily architectural watercolor.

**How the money works.** *(Real figures being researched now: per-unit print
cost in Japan vs China at runs of 100/300/500, consignment splits at UTRECHT /
Book and Sons / flotsam books, Tokyo Art Book Fair booth cost and realistic
sell-through, and what comparable self-published hardcovers sell for.)*

The structural point, independent of the numbers: **a print run is the only item
on this entire list with a fixed, knowable cost.** Everything else is an
application and a wait. This is the one thing she can decide to do and know in
advance what it will cost.

Note also: printing in China is dramatically cheaper than in Japan for art
books, and she is Chinese, reads the language, and has the network. That is a
concrete cost advantage the system has been treating as biography.

**Paint by numbers.**
1. Choose the run size — the cost curve is steep between 100 and 300 and flat
   after that.
2. Get quotes from both a Japanese and a Chinese printer for the same spec.
3. Cover the run through channels that don't depend on her audience:
   consignment at three shops and one fair booth. If those cover it, every
   direct sale is upside rather than a load-bearing assumption.
4. Apply to Tokyo Art Book Fair when the call opens (~spring).

**Where she stands.** *Colour Diary* (2021), eight zines, and the one time this
app ever moved her to click through to anything, it was the Tokyo Art Book Fair.

**Chart:** unit cost against run size (the economies-of-scale curve), and
break-even copies by channel with direct sales excluded from the base case.

**Someone living it:** Urbanowicz again — his city books outsell most gallery
careers.

## 5. On Assignment
*Steady client work. The only one with a predictable monthly number.*

**The life.** Editorial illustration, book covers, commissions. Someone briefs
you, you deliver, you're paid on a schedule. Less romantic than the others and
the only one that pays rent reliably. Architecture and atmosphere are in demand
for book covers specifically — literary fiction leans heavily on exactly this
kind of image.

**How the money works.** Per-commission fees, negotiated per use. An agent takes
25–30% and brings work you wouldn't otherwise see. Rates vary enormously by
client, which is why the portfolio you show determines the work you're offered.

**Paint by numbers.**
1. Make a portfolio that shows the *use*, not the paintings — a cover mock-up
   reads to an art director in a way a gallery image doesn't.
2. Enter the industry's own competitions — The Choice, HB Gallery FILE. These
   are where Japanese art directors actually look.
3. Approach publishers directly; Japanese literary publishers commission covers
   constantly.
4. An agent only after there's work to manage, not before.

**Where she stands.** Her training is in illustration and design, which is
precisely this world — but there's no commercial work on record. The distance
here is short and mostly about presentation.

**Someone living it:** Tatsuro Kiuchi — atmospheric spaces, covers and editorial,
one of the most-commissioned illustrators in Japan.
