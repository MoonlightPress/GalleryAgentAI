## 1. INTERNATIONAL PRINT-ON-DEMAND

### 1.1 First, the structural split that the brief's platform list conceals

The seven platforms named are **two completely different businesses**, and mixing them produces nonsense numbers.

| Model | Platforms | Who is the seller? | Who pays her? | Does she need a shop? | Ongoing work |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Marketplace** | INPRNT, Society6, Redbubble, Displate, Fine Art America | **The platform.** It owns the customer. | The platform, monthly, in USD | No | **Zero after upload** |
| **Supplier / white-label** | **Printful, Printify, Gelato** | **She is.** They are a printing subcontractor. | Nobody — *she pays them* | **Yes** — Etsy, Shopify, BASE, Squarespace | **Continuous** |

This is why "royalty rate" is meaningless for Printful and Printify: **there is no royalty.** Her take is retail − product cost − shipping − her storefront's fees, and she absorbs every refund and every customer email. Under the brief's ranking rule, that alone pushes the supplier model down the list regardless of margin.

#### The W-8BEN question, answered

**Society6 states the requirement outright.** [Introducing Trolley](https://help.society6.com/en-US/introducing-trolley-our-new-artist-payment-platform-7503511), **DIRECT** verbatim:

> *"Federal regulations require Society6 to maintain a valid tax form for every artist receiving earnings. U.S. artists should complete a **W-9**. Non-U.S. artists should complete a **W-8**… If a valid tax form is not submitted, we may be legally required to withhold taxes from future payouts as required by law."*

**W-8BEN asks for country of citizenship *and* country of residence, and the treaty claim is made on residence.** This is where being a Chinese citizen living in Japan is worth real money. From the IRS's own treaty table — [irs.gov/pub/irs-lbi/tax-treaty-table-1.pdf](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-lbi/tax-treaty-table-1.pdf), **Table 1, page 4, "Royalties", column "Copyrights"** — **DIRECT**:

| Treaty country | Industrial equipment | Know-how/other | Patents | Film & TV | **Copyrights** | Treaty article |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Japan** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **0%** | **12(1)** |
| **China, People's Rep. of** | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | **10%** | 11(2) |

> **A Japan-resident claiming treaty benefits pays 0% US withholding on copyright royalties. A China-resident pays 10%.** On ¥100,000 of INPRNT earnings that is a ¥10,000 difference, every year, for the cost of ticking the right box.

⚠️ **Two honest caveats.** (1) Whether a POD platform classifies her earnings as *royalties* (Income Code 12) or as *business profits* is a platform-by-platform question this report did not resolve — Society6 says only "W-8", not which sub-form. (2) The IRS page carries its own warning: *"These tables… are not meant to be a complete guide to all provisions of every income tax treaty."* This is a real, sourced, material fact — **it is not tax advice, and the form itself should be filled in with a professional or with the platform's own guidance.**

🔴 **GAP — INPRNT publishes no tax-form requirement anywhere.** **Tried:** the full [artist agreement](https://www.inprnt.com/info/artist_terms/) (searched every occurrence of "tax", "W-8", "W-9", "withhold" — the only hits are a boilerplate mention of a guardian's "tax information" and the phrase *"pay the remainder (your 'earnings') to you less any taxes, fees…"*), the entire [Selling Products help category](https://help.inprnt.com/category/56-category), and the payout article. INPRNT is a US entity — the shipping page identifies it as **"INPRNT (Deviathan LLC)"** — so a W-8 is likely, but **UNVERIFIED**. Same for Redbubble and Fine Art America.

## 3. HOW ANY OF THIS ACTUALLY GETS SOLD

### 3.1 What is already settled, and is not re-litigated here

**§5 of `book_economics_2026-09-05.md` established that no reliable follower→sales conversion benchmark exists.** Across 27 art-book Kickstarters not one published a follower count; where a real follower count could be paired with a real buyer count, conversion ranged **0.26% to 15.8%**, a 60× spread that ran *the wrong way* with audience size; and every named Japanese source that addressed the question directly said follower count does not predict copies.

**Nothing found for this report contradicts that, and one thing in §3.3 below looks like it does until you read the denominator.** The distinction that keeps both reports consistent:

> **§5 of the book report is about `followers → buyers`. Everything documented in §3.3 is about `store visitors → buyers`.** These are different ratios with different denominators. A store-visitor conversion rate says nothing about how many followers become visitors — and that first step is exactly the one nobody has measured.

### 3.2 ⭐ Email lists — the only place in this whole area with a large, disclosed dataset

This is the strongest evidence base in Part 3, and it is not close. **Klaviyo's 2026 benchmarks, drawn from a stated sample of over 183,000 brands** — [klaviyo.com/uk/blog/email-marketing-benchmarks](https://www.klaviyo.com/uk/blog/email-marketing-benchmarks-open-click-and-conversion-rates), all **DIRECT** and verbatim:

| Metric | One-off campaigns | Automated flows |
|---|---|---|
| Average open rate | **31%** (top 10%: 45.1%) | — |
| Average click rate | **1.69%** (top 10%: 3.38%) | **5.58%** (top 10%: 10.48%) |
| **Average placed-order rate** | **0.16%** (top 10%: 0.36%) | **2.11%** (top 10%: 4.3%) |
| "Toys & hobbies" placed-order rate (nearest sector to art) | **0.19%** | 2.14% |
| Clothing & accessories | 0.12% | 2.15% |

Klaviyo's own summary: *"Automated flows significantly outperform campaigns, delivering around **3x higher click rates and 13x higher placed order rates**, and generating nearly **41% of email revenue from just 5.3% of sends**."*

> ### **What this means for a mailing list she might actually build**
> **A one-off "new prints are up" email to a 1,000-person list produces, on average, 1.6 orders.** (0.16% × 1,000.) To a 5,000-person list, 8 orders. **That is the documented, large-sample reality of a broadcast newsletter, and it is much smaller than the folklore.**
>
> **The 13× gap between campaigns and automated flows is the real finding.** A welcome sequence that fires once, forever, when somebody signs up, converts at **2.11%** — thirteen times a broadcast — and it is the only marketing asset in this entire report that keeps working with **zero ongoing input**. Under the brief's ranking rule, a one-time-setup welcome flow outranks every recurring campaign, and it outranks them by an order of magnitude.
>
> ⚠️ **Two caveats stated plainly.** These are ecommerce brands, not artists — no art-specific cut exists in the dataset. And Klaviyo's own disclaimer applies to the open rate: *"Open rate isn't 100% effective because Apple 'opens' each email for any user with an enabled Apple MPP."* The **placed-order rate is not affected by that** and is the number to use.

**She already has the tool, free.** BASE's **メールマガジン App** ([#41](https://apps.thebase.in/detail/41), **DIRECT**) sends to past buyers who opted in *and* to anyone who signs up through a form on her shop page, using six templates, on a schedule. **⚠️ Its limits, verbatim:** 「メールマガジンの配信回数は、**1日3回まで**」 and new subscribers 「配信対象への反映は、**翌日以降**となります」. 🔴 **BASE's newsletter App does not appear to offer automated welcome flows** — the help text describes only scheduled broadcasts. **If the Klaviyo finding above is the important one, BASE's free app delivers the weaker half of it.** This was not verified beyond the app description page. **UNVERIFIED.**

### 3.3 Timed drops versus an always-open shop — read the sourcing carefully

The one organisation publishing numbers here is **theprintspace**, a London fine-art printer **that sells drop-management services to artists**. Their figures are DIRECT reads of their own pages and are tagged **VENDOR CLAIM** throughout, because they profit if you believe them.

From [How do artists increase their online art sales?](https://www.theprintspace.co.uk/help/knowledge-base/how-do-artists-increase-their-online-art-sales/), **DIRECT quotation, VENDOR CLAIM as evidence**:

> *"The objective is to get your conversion rate up to a minimum of **1.7%**, ideally up to **2.5%**… in some of the print drops that we've been doing for our clients, they've been selling hundreds of thousands of dollars worth of art a year, but we've doubled that with timed drops. So they were converting at **0.5-0.7%** and what we've done with timed drops has increased that conversion rate up to 1.7%."*

From [Selling art prints in time-limited drops](https://www.theprintspace.co.uk/help/knowledge-base/selling-art-prints-in-timed-drops/), **DIRECT quotation, VENDOR CLAIM**:

> *"Even if you only have **400 followers**, if they are genuine and highly engaged followers then you could sell up to **4-5 prints** every time you do a print drop, if you do it right. This is based on the conversion rate data we see across all artists we work with."*

> ### ⚠️ How to hold these two numbers
> **The 1.7% is a store-visitor conversion rate.** It does not contradict §5 of the book report, because §5 is about followers, not visitors. It is a plausible, specific, art-specific figure — and the sample size, the number of artists, and the methodology are **all undisclosed**.
>
> **The "4-5 prints from 400 followers" claim is a different animal and should be treated with far more suspicion.** It *is* a follower→buyer ratio (≈1.1%), it comes from a company selling drop services, no dataset is shown, and it sits inside the exact 0.26%–15.8% spread that §5 showed carries no predictive power. **It is a marketing claim in the shape of a benchmark. Do not budget against it.**

**Their mechanical advice is more useful than their numbers**, and is at least internally consistent (**DIRECT**, [Improve the conversion rates of your online art store](https://www.theprintspace.co.uk/help/knowledge-base/improve-conversion-rate-of-online-art-store/)): a drop landing page should be *"a single page with no escape so all you can do is buy. You can't even click the logo"*; the artwork large; a countdown; the scale visible as the size option changes; *"friction in the checkout process is just an absolute conversion killer."* On re-selling a dropped edition they recommend *"at least 9 months, but usually more than 12 months before you offer it again."* And on ordering the pre-drop posts: *"start by talking about the meaning or the inspiration behind the body of work… then… your creative process, and then talk about the print, show the print."*

**Independent, non-vendor support for scarcity is thin but real.** Shopify's [Limited Drops](https://www.shopify.com/blog/limited-drops) piece cites *a 2025 University of Osaka study* finding limited-time products lift sales of regular items, and *a Journal of Retailing* finding that limited quantities create competitive urgency. 🔴 **Neither primary study was retrieved or read for this report** — they are cited here as *"Shopify says these studies exist"*, which is **UNVERIFIED** and should not be quoted as a finding.

🔴 **GAP — there is no documented head-to-head comparison of a timed drop against an always-open shop for an independent artist.** **Tried:** searches in English and Japanese for artist case studies with before/after numbers; theprintspace's full knowledge base; Shopify's drop literature. **The honest answer is that nobody has published this.** Every source arguing for drops is either selling drop services or generalising from fashion retail. **Under the brief's ranking rule this matters twice over: a drop is a recurring campaign with a deadline, i.e. exactly the high-ongoing-work pattern that ranks lowest for her, and the evidence that it outperforms an always-open shop is a vendor's word.**

**What she can do for free if she ever wants to test it:** BASE ships **販売期間設定** (#79), **予約販売** (#86) and **抽選販売** (#88) as free apps. The infrastructure costs nothing; only her attention does.

### 3.4 Process and timelapse video — the format data says the opposite of the folklore

**Socialinsider's Instagram benchmarks, from a stated sample of 35 million Instagram posts across 447,613 accounts** — [socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram), **DIRECT**:

| Format | Engagement rate 2024 | 2025 | **Q1 2026** | **Q2 2026** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carousels** | 0.55% | 0.55% | 0.52% | **0.50% — still the highest** |
| **Reels** | 0.50% | 0.52% | 0.50% | **0.48%** |
| **Images** | 0.45% | 0.37% | 0.35% | **0.33%** |
| Platform overall | — | — | — | **0.45%** |

Their own verbatim summary: *"Instagram performance tightened in 2025, with engagement **down ~24% YoY**… **Carousels remain the most effective format**… **Reels have become table stakes**, offering stable performance but no longer guaranteeing incremental gains. **Static images continue to decline**… Brands started to post more Reels — **33% increase YoY**."*

> ### **What this actually says about timelapse video**
> **Video does not beat a carousel. It never quite has, in this dataset, in any quarter shown.** Reels are 0.48% against carousels' 0.50%. What video *does* clearly beat is a **single static image (0.33%)** — a 45% gap that has widened every quarter.
>
> **The practical translation for a painter: the documented uplift comes from posting more than one frame, not from posting video.** A carousel of five stages of the same painting outperforms one finished photograph, and does so without her learning to edit video or spending an evening on a timelapse. That is a genuinely low-ongoing-work finding, and it is backed by the largest disclosed sample in this report's Part 3.
>
> ⚠️ **Engagement is not a purchase.** The book report §5.4 already established that, and it holds here. These figures say what gets *seen*, not what gets *bought*. Nobody has published the second half.

🔴 **GAP — no data at all was found on whether process video converts to art sales.** **Tried:** searches for artist case studies pairing timelapse posting with sales figures, in English and Japanese. Not one exists in the reachable literature. **The claim that process video sells art is entirely folklore.**
